## The problem

The body's response to a sensor limits its accuracy and its wear life. In mice, an implanted continuous glucose monitor took 24 minutes to reach its peak signal, against 2 minutes for blood glucose. Over 80 percent of that delay came from the fibrous capsule around the sensor (McClatchey 2019). The chart above shows it. The capsule shows up as lag, drift and lost sensitivity. One implantable sensor controls the tissue response with a steroid-eluting collar. That is a finite drug reservoir.

## What ZwiCoat does here

The results below are from published studies on silicone and on implant arrays.

**Scar capsule, in mice.** Around coated silicone sheets at six weeks the capsule was 27 micrometers, against 82 uncoated. Around human cochlear implant arrays at one year it was about 60 percent thinner, 47 against 18 micrometers, including over platinum contacts. The film was intact at one year. (Horne 2023.)

{{figure:W27}}

**Cells and fibrosis, in sheep.** At the round window of the sheep cochlea, coated arrays had fewer cell nuclei, fewer macrophages and less fibrosis than uncoated arrays when the three sheep were pooled. (Ben-Shlomo 2026.)

{{figure:W26}}

Related work points the same way. A different zwitterionic coating cut glucose sensor error without recalibration from 73 to 17 percent in mice over three days (Xie 2018). ZwiCoat is a thicker, crosslinked, grafted hydrogel.

## How it fits your process

One coating step goes onto the sensor body. The published silicone films were about 7 micrometers thick (Leigh 2019). A sensor needs a thin film that lets glucose through, and the film is tunable: the crosslinker level sets how stiff and how swollen the gel is.

Your bench can measure sensor response time on the coated parts. The film is drug-free.
